TITLE: Ringworm in Cats: Identifying It, Treating It, and Keeping Yourself Safe

TAGS: ringworm, skin, fungal, zoonotic, kittens

First things first: ringworm isn't a worm. It's a fungal infection (the medical name is dermatophytosis), and the name comes from the classic circular shape it sometimes makes on the skin. It's one of the most common skin issues in cats, especially kittens and cats from shelters or multi-cat environments.

The classic look is a round patch of hair loss, often with crusty, flaky, or red skin in the middle. But ringworm doesn't always read the textbook — it can also show up as just thinning fur, scabby spots, broken hairs, or generalized scruffiness. Common spots are the face, ears, paws, and tail, but it can pop up anywhere. Some cats (especially long-haired breeds) can carry ringworm without obvious symptoms, which is part of why it spreads so easily.

Here's the part that catches a lot of new cat owners off guard: ringworm is contagious to humans. It's zoonotic, meaning it jumps between species, and you can absolutely get it from your cat — usually as itchy red rings on your arms, neck, or wherever the cat snuggles. Kids and people with weaker immune systems are more susceptible. Don't panic, it's very treatable in humans (over-the-counter antifungal creams usually do the job), but you do need to take it seriously.

If you suspect ringworm, get to the vet for a proper diagnosis. They'll usually do one or more of: a Wood's lamp exam (a UV light that makes some ringworm species glow), a fungal culture (the gold standard, but takes 1-3 weeks), or a quick microscopic exam of plucked hairs. Don't try to diagnose it yourself by appearance alone — plenty of other conditions look similar, and treatment differs.

Treatment usually involves topical antifungal medication (creams or medicated baths/dips) plus oral antifungal pills for several weeks. Mild cases sometimes resolve on their own in healthy adult cats, but treating actively shortens the illness and reduces the risk of spreading it. Don't stop treatment early just because the cat looks better — ringworm is sneaky and can come back.

Environmental cleanup is the part most people underestimate. Ringworm spores survive in the environment for up to 18 months, hiding in carpet, bedding, furniture, and HVAC vents. While treating your cat, you'll want to vacuum frequently (and toss the bag), wash bedding in hot water, wipe hard surfaces with a diluted bleach solution where safe, and ideally confine the infected cat to one easy-to-clean room until they're cleared. In multi-cat households, all cats usually need to be checked — even the ones that look fine.

Wear gloves when handling the affected cat, wash your hands thoroughly, and keep an eye on your own skin. If you see suspicious red rings on yourself, see a doctor or pharmacist. The whole process takes patience — usually 4-8 weeks of consistent treatment — but ringworm is very curable. It just demands you stay on it.
