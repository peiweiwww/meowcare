TITLE: Wet Food vs. Dry Food vs. Raw: How to Choose What to Feed Your Cat

CATEGORY: nutrition
TAGS: feeding, wet-food, dry-food, raw-diet, hydration
---

The wet vs. dry food debate is one of those things that can feel really fraught online, with strong opinions in every direction. Here's the practical reality: both can work, both have trade-offs, and the best diet is usually one your cat actually eats and that fits your life.

Wet food's biggest advantage is moisture. Cats evolved as desert animals and have a notoriously low thirst drive — they're supposed to get most of their water from prey. Wet food is roughly 70-80% water, which closely matches what their body is built for. This is especially valuable for cats prone to urinary issues, kidney disease, or who just don't drink much. Wet food also tends to be higher in protein and lower in carbs, which more closely matches a cat's natural diet (cats are obligate carnivores — they're literally built to eat meat). The downsides: it's more expensive, can't be left out all day, and creates more dishes to wash.

Dry food's advantages are practical: it's cheaper, shelf-stable, can be left out for free-feeding, and doesn't need refrigeration. The downsides matter though. It's typically only about 10% moisture, so cats eating dry-only diets often live in a state of mild chronic dehydration without anyone noticing. It also tends to be higher in carbs, which isn't ideal for an obligate carnivore — and can contribute to obesity in cats who free-feed. The myth that dry food cleans teeth is mostly just that, a myth: most kibble shatters too easily to scrape teeth. Specific dental diets are the exception.

The compromise that works for a lot of cats: a mix. Wet food for at least one meal a day (more if your cat will eat it), with measured dry food for the rest. This gets you the moisture benefits of wet food, the convenience of dry, and helps with portion control. If you only do one, wet food is generally the better choice nutritionally, but a cat who refuses wet food and thrives on dry is going to be fine too.

About raw diets: this is where things get controversial. Proponents argue raw is the most natural, biologically appropriate diet. Mainstream veterinary organizations including the AVMA and the FDA generally don't recommend raw diets due to risks of bacterial contamination (salmonella, E. coli, listeria) for both cats and humans in the household, plus the difficulty of getting the nutrient balance exactly right. If you're committed to raw feeding, work with a veterinary nutritionist to formulate a balanced diet — homemade raw diets without expert guidance frequently end up nutritionally deficient. Commercial frozen or freeze-dried raw products that have been HPP (high pressure processed) are somewhat safer but still carry more risk than cooked food.

What matters most when picking a food: look for the AAFCO statement on the label saying it's "complete and balanced" for your cat's life stage (kitten, adult, senior, or "all life stages"). This means it meets minimum nutritional standards — without it, you're basically guessing. Beyond that, look at the protein source (named meats like "chicken" are better than "meat by-products"), avoid foods with a lot of fillers, and match the food to your cat's life stage and any health conditions.

If you're switching foods, do it gradually over 7-10 days — mix increasing amounts of the new food with decreasing amounts of the old. Cats can be picky, and sudden changes often cause vomiting or diarrhea. And when in doubt, your vet can help you pick something that fits your specific cat's needs.
