TITLE: Cat Vomiting: When It's Normal and When to Call the Vet
SOURCES: https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/vomiting
CATEGORY: health
TAGS: vomiting, digestive, emergency-signs, hairballs
---

Cats throw up. It's one of those things that comes with the territory of cat ownership, and most of the time it's not a big deal. But sometimes it is — and knowing the difference can save you a panicked midnight Google search (or your cat's life).

The most common reasons for occasional vomiting are pretty mundane: hairballs, eating too fast, or a sudden diet change. If your cat tosses up a hairball every couple of weeks, or throws up right after wolfing down dinner, that's usually nothing to worry about. Slowing them down with a puzzle feeder or switching to smaller, more frequent meals often takes care of it. Hairballs can be reduced with regular brushing and hairball-control food.

What you actually want to watch for are patterns. Vomiting more than two or three times in 24 hours, vomiting that keeps going for more than a day, or vomiting paired with other symptoms — that's when it shifts from "ugh, gross" to "we need a vet." The other symptoms matter a lot here. A cat who throws up once and then happily demands dinner is fine. A cat who throws up and then hides under the bed, refuses food, seems weak, or has diarrhea is telling you something is wrong.

Some red flags mean you should go to the vet immediately, not wait and see. Blood in the vomit (either bright red or what looks like coffee grounds) is one. Repeated unproductive retching — your cat is hunched over trying to throw up but nothing comes out — can signal a blockage, which is a true emergency. If your cat hasn't eaten or drunk anything in 24 hours, especially combined with vomiting, that's also urgent because cats can develop a serious liver condition called hepatic lipidosis surprisingly fast when they stop eating.

Kittens, senior cats, and cats with existing health conditions like diabetes or kidney disease have less margin for error. What might be a "wait and see" situation in a healthy adult cat is often a "call the vet today" situation in these guys. Chronic vomiting — more than once a week for several weeks — is also never just a quirk; it usually points to something like inflammatory bowel disease, hyperthyroidism, or kidney disease, all of which are very treatable when caught early.

When in doubt, call. Most vet clinics are happy to do a quick phone triage to help you decide whether to come in. And one last thing: keep an eye on what's coming up. Color, frequency, whether it contains food or just liquid or foam — your vet will ask, and "I don't remember, I was too busy cleaning the rug" isn't super helpful. A quick photo on your phone before you clean it up feels weird but actually really helps.
