TITLE: Bringing Home a New Cat: The First Week

The first week with a new cat is a weird mix of excitement and worry. You probably imagined cuddles and zoomies on day one, and instead your cat has been hiding under the bed for 36 hours and you're wondering if you've ruined their life. Good news: this is completely normal, and the slow start is actually how it's supposed to go.

**Set up a safe room before they arrive.** Don't let a new cat have run of the whole house immediately — it's overwhelming and they'll often pick a hiding spot you can't reach (behind the fridge, inside a couch). Pick one room (a bedroom or bathroom works well) and stock it with: a litter box, food and water bowls, a comfortable bed or two, a few hiding spots (cardboard boxes are great), a scratching post, and some toys. This becomes their home base for the first few days while they adjust.

**The first 24-48 hours, expect hiding.** Most cats will find a corner, under the bed, or behind furniture and stay there. Don't force them out, don't constantly try to pet them, don't panic. Sit in the room quietly, talk softly, let them come to you. Leave food and water out and check the litter box for signs of life (you want to see them eating, drinking, and using the box, even if they're doing it when you're not watching). It's totally normal for a new cat to barely eat for the first day — stress affects appetite. If they haven't eaten by 48 hours, call the vet.

**Days 3-7: cautious exploration.** Most cats start to come out and investigate, sometimes when you're asleep, then gradually when you're around. You'll see their personality start to emerge. Some cats are bold and want to be friends within a few days; others take weeks to fully relax. Both are normal. Resist the urge to introduce them to the whole house too fast — once they're comfortable in the safe room, expand gradually, one room at a time.

**The 3-3-3 rule** is a useful framework that's worth knowing: 3 days to start feeling safe, 3 weeks to settle into routines and start showing real personality, 3 months to fully bond and feel completely at home. Most cats follow some version of this timeline. If your cat is still very shy at week 4, don't worry — keep being patient, keep the routine consistent, keep offering positive interactions on their terms.

**Vet visit within the first week or two** is a good idea, even if your cat seems healthy. You want a baseline exam, vaccinations updated if needed, parasite check (intestinal parasites are common in shelter and rescue cats), and any health questions answered. If you adopted from a shelter, bring whatever paperwork they gave you. If your cat is hiding and stressed, schedule it for a few days out so they have time to start settling first.

**Things people don't think about but should:**
- Hide cords, secure blinds (looped pull cords are a strangulation hazard), check that screens on windows are secure, and remove any toxic plants. Cats explore by climbing, chewing, and squeezing into tiny spaces.
- Identify all the small openings in your house (behind the washer, gap under the stove, opening into the floor heating vent). New cats can find their way into surprisingly tight spots when scared.
- If you have other pets, plan introductions slowly — they shouldn't meet face-to-face on day one. Use scent swapping (rub a towel on each, place in the other's space) for several days, then gradual visual introductions through a barrier, then short supervised meetings. This process can take weeks. Rushing it usually creates lasting tension.
- Microchip your cat if they aren't already, and update the registration with your contact info. New cats are flight risks, especially during the adjustment period — don't let them outside, even on a leash, for at least a few weeks.

**Don't take rejection personally.** Some cats fall in love with their new humans on day one. Others act like you're a stranger holding them captive for weeks. Both will eventually become loving cats — they just have different processing speeds. Your job is to be the consistent, calm, treat-providing person who never forces interaction. Most cats come around in their own time, and the bond is stronger for it.

**A few specific signs to call the vet about:** not eating or drinking after 48 hours, no urination or stool in the litter box after 48 hours, vomiting or diarrhea, sneezing and goopy eyes (URI is super common in shelter cats), or any visible injury or limping. Otherwise, most "weird" behavior in the first week is just stress and will resolve.
