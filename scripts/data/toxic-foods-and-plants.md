TITLE: Foods and Plants That Are Dangerous for Cats
SOURCES: https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/poisons
CATEGORY: nutrition
TAGS: toxic-foods, poisoning, lilies, emergency, household-hazards
---

Cats are smaller than us, metabolize things differently, and a surprising number of common household foods and plants are dangerous to them. Here's what to keep away from your cat — and what to do if they get into something they shouldn't.

**Lilies are the big one.** True lilies (genus Lilium) and daylilies (Hemerocallis) are extremely toxic to cats. Even tiny amounts — a few licks of pollen off their fur, drinking water from a vase, chewing on a leaf — can cause acute kidney failure within 24-72 hours. This is one of the most serious household hazards for cats, and a lot of cat owners don't know about it. If you have cats, don't bring lilies into your home, and if someone sends you a bouquet, check what's in it. If your cat has been around lilies in any way, get to an emergency vet immediately, even if they seem fine. Treatment is much more effective when started early.

**Other dangerous plants** include sago palms (extremely toxic, can cause liver failure), tulips and hyacinths (especially the bulbs), azaleas and rhododendrons, oleander, and many common houseplants like dieffenbachia, philodendron, and pothos. The ASPCA maintains a comprehensive list of toxic and non-toxic plants on their website — worth checking before bringing any new plant home.

**Dangerous human foods:**
- **Onions, garlic, leeks, chives, and shallots** — all cause damage to red blood cells and can lead to anemia. This includes powdered or cooked forms, so be careful with leftover pasta sauces, soups, and seasoned meats.
- **Chocolate** — toxic due to theobromine. Dark chocolate is worse than milk chocolate, but no chocolate is safe.
- **Grapes and raisins** — known to cause kidney failure in dogs; the toxicity in cats is less well-studied but it's safest to avoid.
- **Alcohol** — even small amounts are dangerous. Cats are much smaller than us and a few sips of beer or wine can cause serious problems.
- **Caffeine** — coffee, tea, energy drinks. Can cause heart issues and seizures.
- **Xylitol** — a sugar substitute found in sugar-free gum, peanut butter, baked goods, and increasingly toothpaste. Highly toxic to dogs; less data for cats but better avoided.
- **Raw bread dough** — yeast continues to ferment in the warm stomach, producing alcohol and causing painful bloating.
- **Macadamia nuts** — well-documented toxicity in dogs; avoid for cats.
- **Raw fish (regularly)** — contains an enzyme that destroys thiamine. Occasional small amounts are fine, but a regular raw fish diet causes serious deficiency.

**About milk and cheese:** the cartoon image of cats lapping cream is misleading. Most adult cats are lactose intolerant and dairy gives them diarrhea or stomach upset. A tiny lick of yogurt or cheese probably won't hurt, but milk shouldn't be a regular thing.

**About bones:** cooked bones (especially chicken) can splinter and cause choking, mouth injuries, or intestinal punctures. Raw bones are debated — some raw-feeding advocates argue they're safe, but they still come with bacterial contamination and dental fracture risks.

**Other household hazards** worth knowing about: many human medications (acetaminophen/Tylenol is fatal to cats even in small doses, ibuprofen is also very dangerous), essential oils (especially tea tree, eucalyptus, citrus, peppermint, pine — many are toxic via skin contact or diffusing in the air), antifreeze (ethylene glycol, sweet tasting and lethal even in tiny amounts), rodent poison, and household cleaners.

**If you think your cat has been poisoned:** don't try to make them vomit unless specifically directed by a vet — some substances cause more damage coming back up. Call your vet, an emergency vet, or one of these poison control hotlines:

- **ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center: 888-426-4435** (consultation fee applies)
- **Pet Poison Helpline: 855-764-7661** (consultation fee applies)

Both are available 24/7. The fee is worth it — they can tell you what's actually toxic, what's a non-issue, and what to do next. Have the substance/packaging ready when you call so you can describe what was ingested. And save these numbers in your phone now, before you need them.
