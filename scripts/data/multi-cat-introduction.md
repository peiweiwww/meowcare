TITLE: Introducing a New Cat to Your Resident Cat (Without World War III)

CATEGORY: care
TAGS: multi-cat, introductions, scent-swapping, territory, conflict

Adding a second cat to a household with an existing cat is one of those things that can go really well or really badly, and a lot of how it goes depends on how you handle the first few weeks. The single biggest mistake people make is moving too fast. The second biggest is moving too fast. (The third is also moving too fast.)

**Why slow introductions matter:** cats are territorial. To a resident cat, your home isn't just where they live — it's their entire territory, marked with their scent, organized to their preferences. A new cat suddenly appearing in that space is, from their perspective, a hostile invasion. First impressions stick. A bad first meeting can create tension that lasts for years; a good slow introduction usually leads to peaceful coexistence and sometimes genuine friendship.

**Phase 1: Total separation (3-7 days, sometimes longer).** New cat goes in a separate room with the door closed. Their own food, water, litter box, beds, toys. The two cats should not see each other yet. They will hear and smell each other, which is the whole point — they're getting used to the idea that another cat exists in this space, without any pressure to interact. Watch the resident cat: hissing or growling at the door is normal. What you want to see over time is curiosity replacing aggression — sniffing under the door, sitting near it without hissing.

**Phase 2: Scent swapping.** Take a soft cloth or sock and rub it on each cat's cheeks (where their friendly scent glands are), then place it in the other cat's space. Swap food bowls between rooms (after meals are done) so they associate the other cat's smell with eating. Some people also rotate the cats — let the new cat explore the main living areas (with the resident cat shut in another room) for short periods. This lets the new cat get familiar with the house and lets the resident cat smell the new cat's presence in their territory. Do this for several days.

**Phase 3: Visual contact through a barrier.** A baby gate (or two stacked) or a cracked-open door with a doorstop lets them see each other without being able to touch. Some people use a screen door. Feed both cats at the same time on opposite sides of the barrier — they're eating, which is positive, while seeing the other cat. Start with a lot of distance and slowly move bowls closer over multiple meals. If either cat gets too tense to eat, you've moved too fast; back up.

**Phase 4: Supervised meetings.** Once visual contact is calm and they can eat near each other through the barrier, allow short face-to-face sessions. Keep them brief (5-10 minutes initially) and end on a positive note — before either cat gets stressed. Have a towel or thick blanket nearby in case you need to safely separate them (never use bare hands to break up a cat fight). Repeat these sessions, gradually extending the time. If there's hissing, swatting, or one cat is clearly stressed, take a step back to the previous phase for a few more days.

**Phase 5: Full integration.** Eventually, when supervised meetings are consistently calm, you can leave them together unsupervised for short periods, then longer, then full-time. Some cats reach this stage in 2-3 weeks; others take 2-3 months. A few never become best friends and just learn to peacefully ignore each other, and that's also a successful outcome.

**The resource problem.** Cat conflict is often actually resource conflict. Make sure you have:
- Multiple food and water stations in different locations (so one cat can't guard them)
- Enough litter boxes — one per cat plus one extra is the rule, in different rooms (a cat ambushing another at the litter box is a recipe for litter box avoidance)
- Multiple cozy spots, scratching posts, and elevated perches (vertical territory is huge for cats — they're way more comfortable when they have high spots to retreat to)
- Enough toys and attention to go around

**Warning signs that introductions aren't working** (yet, or maybe at all): one cat is constantly hiding and not eating, persistent fighting (not just hissing or batting — actual fighting with biting), inappropriate elimination outside the litter box (especially in places like beds or laundry), or one cat is clearly being terrorized. If you see these, slow down, go back a phase, and consider consulting your vet or a feline behaviorist. Sometimes Feliway (a calming pheromone diffuser) helps. Sometimes the cats just aren't compatible, but this is rarer than people think when introductions are done patiently.

**Honest reality check:** not all cats will end up cuddling. Many adult cats who are introduced will live in the same house perfectly peacefully without ever being close friends — they'll have their own zones, ignore each other, and that's fine. If you want guaranteed snuggle buddies, adopting a bonded pair from a shelter (cats who are already friends and are adopted together) is the most reliable path.
