TITLE: Cat Diarrhea: What's Causing It and When to Worry
SOURCES: https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/diarrhea
CATEGORY: health
TAGS: diarrhea, digestive, dehydration, kittens
---

Soft stool, runny stool, or full-on liquid disasters in the litter box — diarrhea is one of those things that can mean almost nothing or something pretty serious, and the trick is knowing which one you're dealing with.

A single bout of soft stool in an otherwise happy, eating, drinking cat is usually no big deal. The most common culprits are diet-related: a sudden food change, a treat their stomach didn't agree with, eating something they found on the floor, or stress (yes, cats get stress diarrhea, especially after travel, vet visits, or new pets in the house). In these cases, things usually settle down within a day or two on their own.

But diarrhea that lasts more than 48 hours, or comes back repeatedly, deserves a vet visit. Persistent diarrhea is a common symptom of intestinal parasites (especially in kittens or outdoor cats), inflammatory bowel disease, food allergies, infections, or thyroid issues. Parasites are worth flagging because they're really common and easy to miss — a fecal sample at the vet usually sorts it out fast.

Watch for the warning signs that turn diarrhea from "annoying" to "urgent": blood in the stool (either bright red or black and tarry), vomiting at the same time as diarrhea, lethargy, refusing to eat, or any sign of dehydration like dry gums or sunken eyes. Kittens are especially vulnerable here — they can dehydrate dangerously fast, sometimes within hours. Same goes for senior cats and cats with existing health problems. If a kitten has diarrhea for more than a few hours, don't wait — call the vet.

If your adult cat has mild diarrhea and seems otherwise fine, a few things can help at home while you monitor. Make sure water is always available (diarrhea is dehydrating). Some vets recommend a 12-hour fast for adult cats followed by small portions of bland food (plain boiled chicken, or a vet-prescribed bland diet) for a day or two before transitioning back. Don't fast kittens though — they need consistent calories. And never give human anti-diarrhea medications like Imodium or Pepto-Bismol; some of them are toxic to cats.

If you're heading to the vet, bring a fresh stool sample if you can (a clean ziplock bag works). It saves time and gets you to a diagnosis faster. And take a mental note of what the diarrhea looks like — color, consistency, any mucus or blood, how often. Those details actually matter for figuring out what's going on.
