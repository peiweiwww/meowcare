TITLE: How to Read Cat Food Labels: Protein, AAFCO, and What Actually Matters
SOURCES:
CATEGORY: nutrition
TAGS: nutrition-labels, AAFCO, protein, taurine, ingredients
---

Cat food labels are designed to be confusing. Marketing is heavy, ingredient lists are long, and the most important information is usually buried in tiny text. Here's what actually matters when you're standing in the pet food aisle trying to figure out what to buy.

The single most important thing to look for is the **AAFCO statement**. AAFCO is the Association of American Feed Control Officials, and their nutritional standards are the closest thing the pet food industry has to a regulatory baseline. Look for a sentence like "formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Cat Food Nutrient Profiles for [life stage]" or "animal feeding tests using AAFCO procedures substantiate that [product] provides complete and balanced nutrition for [life stage]." The feeding-tested version is slightly stronger evidence because it's based on actual cats eating the food, not just a calculation. Either way, no AAFCO statement means you're guessing whether the food is nutritionally complete.

Life stage matters: AAFCO statements specify "growth" (kittens), "maintenance" (adult cats), "all life stages" (covers both), or "gestation/lactation" (pregnant or nursing). Senior cats don't have their own AAFCO category — adult maintenance covers them, with adjustments for specific health conditions.

About protein: cats are obligate carnivores, which means they need substantially more protein than dogs or humans. AAFCO minimum is 26% protein on a dry matter basis for adult maintenance, but most healthy adult cats do better at 35-45%. Kittens need even more, around 30%+. The key phrase is "dry matter basis" — wet food looks lower in protein on the label because of water content, but if you do the math removing the moisture, premium wet foods often have higher protein than dry kibble.

The ingredient list is listed by weight before cooking. The first 3-5 ingredients are most of what's in the food. You want to see specific named meats early in the list — "chicken," "salmon," "turkey," not vague terms like "meat by-products" or "animal digest." Named organ meats ("chicken liver," "chicken heart") are actually great — they're nutrient-dense, despite sounding gross. Be wary of foods with a lot of plant proteins (corn gluten meal, pea protein, soybean meal) padding the protein percentage — cats don't digest plant protein efficiently.

Specific nutrients to make sure are present:
- **Taurine**: an amino acid that cats can't synthesize themselves. Taurine deficiency causes blindness and a fatal heart condition. All AAFCO-compliant cat foods contain it; this is a major reason you should never feed cats dog food (dog food doesn't have to include it).
- **Arachidonic acid**: another fatty acid cats can't make themselves.
- **Vitamin A**: cats need preformed vitamin A from animal sources; they can't convert it from plant sources like beta-carotene.

These are the main reasons cats can't be vegetarians safely without highly specialized supplements, despite occasional claims to the contrary online.

About marketing terms: "natural," "premium," "gourmet," and "human grade" have minimal regulatory definitions and basically mean whatever the manufacturer wants them to mean. "Grain-free" became trendy a few years ago but isn't necessarily better for cats — and in dogs, certain grain-free formulations have been linked to heart problems. For cats specifically, the absence of grain matters less than the overall protein and carb balance. "Organic" has actual USDA standards and is the only one of these terms that means something specific.

If your cat has health issues — kidney disease, diabetes, urinary problems, food allergies — talk to your vet about prescription or therapeutic diets. These foods are formulated specifically to manage those conditions and often work better than over-the-counter alternatives, even if they're more expensive. The cost of food is almost always less than the cost of treating preventable health issues.

One last thing: ignore the photo on the bag. The picture of glistening salmon fillets and fresh vegetables on the front has approximately zero relationship to what's actually in the food. The ingredient list and AAFCO statement on the back are what matter.
