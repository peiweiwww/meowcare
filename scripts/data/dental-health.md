TITLE: Cat Dental Health: Bad Breath, Drooling, and Why Mouth Problems Get Missed
SOURCES: https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-dental-disease
CATEGORY: health
TAGS: dental, gingivitis, bad-breath, drooling, oral
---

Dental disease is one of the most common — and most underdiagnosed — health problems in cats. By the time most cats are 3 years old, they have some degree of dental disease, and most owners have no idea because cats are champions at hiding mouth pain.

The most common issues are gingivitis (red, inflamed gums), periodontal disease (gum disease that affects the structures holding teeth in place), and tooth resorption — a uniquely feline condition where the tooth basically dissolves itself from the inside out. Tooth resorption is genuinely painful and surprisingly common, affecting somewhere between a third and a half of adult cats at some point.

Signs your cat might have a dental problem include: bad breath that's worse than the usual cat-food breath, drooling (especially if it's new), pawing at the mouth, dropping food while eating, eating on one side of the mouth, suddenly preferring soft food over kibble, weight loss, or visible tartar buildup on the teeth (yellow or brown crusty stuff, usually worst on the back teeth). Some cats just become grumpy or withdrawn — chronic mouth pain affects mood, just like it would for us.

Here's the tricky part: many cats with serious dental problems still eat normally and act normal. Cats are wired to hide weakness, and that includes pain. So you can't rely on "well, they're still eating, so they must be fine." Regular vet checkups (which always include an oral exam) are how most dental issues get caught.

Treatment depends on what's going on. Mild gingivitis can sometimes be managed with brushing (yes, you can brush a cat's teeth, more on that below), dental treats, and prescription dental diets. Anything more advanced usually requires a professional dental cleaning under anesthesia — which sounds scary but is the only way to properly clean below the gum line, take dental x-rays, and address things like tooth resorption or extractions. Cleanings without anesthesia (sometimes offered at groomers or pet stores) don't actually clean where the problems are and can give a false sense of security.

About brushing: it's the gold standard for prevention, and easier than you'd think if you start young and go slow. Use a cat-specific toothpaste (never human toothpaste — fluoride is toxic to cats) and a soft brush or finger brush. Start by just letting them lick the toothpaste off your finger, then build up over weeks to actual brushing. Most cats won't tolerate a full dental scrub, but even getting the outside surfaces of the back teeth a few times a week makes a real difference.

If brushing isn't happening, dental treats and chews approved by the Veterinary Oral Health Council (look for the VOHC seal) are the next best thing. Dental diets are designed with kibble shapes and textures that scrape teeth as cats chew. Water additives are mostly low-effort and probably better than nothing.

When to call the vet specifically about teeth: any of the symptoms above, especially drooling, blood in the saliva, or visible swelling. Annual dental checks should be part of your cat's regular vet visits, and if your vet recommends a dental cleaning, take it seriously — leaving advanced dental disease untreated leads to chronic pain, infection, and tooth loss.
