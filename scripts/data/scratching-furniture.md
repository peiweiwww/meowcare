TITLE: Why Cats Scratch Furniture and How to Redirect It
SOURCES: https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-behavior-problems-destructive-behavior

TAGS: scratching, furniture, scratching-posts, declawing, behavior

If your cat is shredding the corner of your couch, the first thing to know is that they're not doing it to spite you. Scratching is one of the most fundamental cat behaviors, hardwired in for a bunch of important reasons, and the goal isn't to stop scratching — it's to redirect it.

**Why cats scratch.** Multiple reasons stack on top of each other: it sheds the dead outer layer of their claws (basically a manicure), it stretches their back and shoulder muscles, it leaves visual and scent marks on their territory (cats have scent glands in their paws), and it just feels good. A cat who isn't scratching is either very sick or very inhibited — both bad. So the question is never "how do I stop my cat from scratching" but "how do I get them to scratch the right things."

**What makes a good scratching surface.** Cats are picky about where they scratch, and most "scratching posts" sold in pet stores are too small, too wobbly, or made of the wrong material. The criteria that actually matter:

- **Tall enough for a full stretch.** A cat should be able to reach up at full extension. Most cats want at least 30 inches; large cats need more. Those tiny 12-inch posts you see at pet stores are useless.
- **Sturdy.** If the post wobbles or tips when scratched, cats abandon it. The base needs to be heavy and wide.
- **The right material.** Most cats prefer sisal rope or sisal fabric. Some prefer cardboard (the corrugated kind, in flat or angled shapes). Carpeted posts are popular at pet stores but actually work badly — the texture is too similar to your carpet, which can confuse the cat about what's okay to scratch.
- **Vertical or horizontal — and ideally both.** Some cats are vertical scratchers (posts), some prefer horizontal (flat cardboard or rugs). Most use both.

**Placement matters.** A scratching post hidden in the basement won't get used. Cats scratch in places that matter to them — near sleeping spots (they often scratch right after waking up to stretch), near doorways and entries, in the middle of social areas. If your cat is scratching the couch, put a post right next to the couch. Once they're using it consistently, you can slowly move it if needed, but start where the action is.

**How to actually redirect a scratcher.** This is where most people give up too early. The protocol that works:

1. Get an appropriate scratching surface (tall, sturdy, sisal or cardboard) and place it next to the furniture they're targeting.
2. Make the furniture less appealing temporarily — double-sided sticky tape (cats hate sticky paws), aluminum foil, plastic sheeting, or commercial deterrent sprays. Citrus scents are unpleasant to most cats. The goal isn't to traumatize them, just to make the wrong spot less fun.
3. Make the post more appealing. Rub catnip on it. Play with toys around it. Praise/treat any time you see them using it.
4. Be patient. It takes a few weeks of consistency, not days.
5. Once they're reliably using the post, you can gradually remove the deterrents from the furniture and (slowly) move the post to a more aesthetically pleasing location if needed.

**Trim claws regularly.** Trimming the very tip of the claws every couple of weeks reduces damage even when scratching happens. Use cat-specific clippers, only trim the clear part (avoid the pink "quick" which is a blood vessel), and start young if possible. If you've never done it, ask your vet to show you — it's much easier with a demo.

**About declawing.** Declawing isn't a nail trim — it's surgical amputation of the last bone of each toe. It's banned in many countries and a growing number of US states and cities for good reason. It causes long-term pain, increases the risk of biting (because the cat lost their primary defense), often causes litter box avoidance (the litter hurts on healing paws), and affects how the cat walks for the rest of their life. The American Veterinary Medical Association strongly discourages it. If you're considering declawing because of furniture damage, please try the redirection methods above first — they really do work with patience. Soft nail caps (like Soft Paws) that glue onto the claws are also a much better alternative for severe cases.

**If redirection isn't working,** consider whether the cat has enough environmental enrichment. Cats who are bored, under-stimulated, or stressed often scratch more. More play time, more vertical space, more interactive toys, and a generally enriched environment usually help.
