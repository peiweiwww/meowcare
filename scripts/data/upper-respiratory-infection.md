TITLE: Cat Sneezing, Runny Nose, and Watery Eyes: Upper Respiratory Infections Explained
SOURCES: https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/respiratory-infections
CATEGORY: health
TAGS: upper-respiratory, sneezing, infection, kittens, shelter-cats
---

If your cat is sneezing, has goopy eyes, a runny nose, or sounds congested, there's a good chance they have what's commonly called a feline upper respiratory infection — basically the cat version of a cold. It's especially common in kittens, cats recently adopted from shelters, and multi-cat households.

The two main troublemakers are feline herpesvirus and feline calicivirus, which together account for most upper respiratory cases. Bacterial infections (like Bordetella or Chlamydia) can also cause it or piggyback on top of a viral infection. The annoying thing about herpesvirus is that once a cat has it, they have it for life — most of the time it stays dormant, but stress, illness, or a weakened immune system can trigger flare-ups. So if your cat seems to get "colds" repeatedly throughout their life, this is often why.

Symptoms range from mild (an occasional sneeze, slightly watery eyes) to obvious (constant sneezing, thick discharge from nose and eyes, congestion you can hear, mouth breathing, drooling, and ulcers on the tongue or gums). Cats often lose their appetite when they're sick — partly because they feel crummy, partly because they can't smell their food when their nose is stuffed. This is a problem because cats who don't eat for more than a day or two can develop serious complications.

Most mild cases resolve on their own in 7-10 days with supportive care at home. Keep them warm, comfortable, and well-hydrated. Wipe discharge from their eyes and nose with a warm damp cloth. Run a humidifier or take them into a steamy bathroom for a few minutes — it really helps loosen congestion. Warm up wet food (smell intensifies, appetite returns) or offer strong-smelling foods like tuna to tempt them to eat.

When to call the vet: if your cat stops eating for more than 24 hours, is having trouble breathing (open-mouth breathing in cats is always a red flag), has thick green or yellow discharge, develops mouth ulcers, or seems lethargic and not improving after a few days. Kittens, senior cats, and cats with other health issues should be seen sooner rather than later — they have less reserve to fight infection. Treatment usually involves antibiotics (for bacterial complications), antiviral medications, eye drops, and sometimes appetite stimulants or fluids.

Prevention is mostly about vaccines and stress management. The core feline vaccines protect against herpesvirus and calicivirus — they don't always prevent infection entirely but they significantly reduce severity. Keeping stress low (consistent routine, hiding spots, slow introductions to new pets or environments) helps prevent flare-ups in cats who already carry herpesvirus. And in multi-cat households, isolating a sick cat as much as possible while they're contagious can save the rest from getting it too.
