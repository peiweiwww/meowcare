TITLE: How Much and How Often to Feed Your Cat at Every Life Stage
SOURCES:
CATEGORY: nutrition
TAGS: feeding, kittens, adult-cats, senior-cats, portion-control
---

How much to feed your cat is one of those questions where the honest answer is "it depends" — but there are decent rules of thumb that work for most cats.

The general daily calorie ballpark: a typical adult indoor cat needs roughly 20 calories per pound of healthy body weight per day. So a 10-pound cat needs around 200 calories. Active outdoor cats need more, couch-potato indoor cats often need less. The calorie content is on every food label, usually as "kcal per cup" for dry food or "kcal per can" for wet food. Do the math, divide into meals, and you have a starting point. From there you adjust based on whether your cat is gaining or losing weight.

Forget the back of the food bag for the most part — those guidelines are usually overestimates, geared toward selling more food. The "amount per cat per day" listed there often turns healthy cats into chubby cats over a year or two. Your vet's recommendation, or a calculation based on calorie needs, is more reliable.

**Kittens (under 1 year)** need a lot of food relative to their body size — they're growing fast. Up to about 4 months old, they should have access to food essentially all the time, or at least 4-5 small meals a day. From 4-6 months, drop to 3-4 meals a day. From 6-12 months, transition to 2-3 meals a day. Always feed kitten-formulated food (it has more protein, fat, and specific nutrients for growth) until they're at least 12 months old. Larger breeds like Maine Coons may need kitten food until 18 months. Don't restrict kitten portions to "prevent obesity" — undernourishing a growing kitten causes real developmental problems.

**Adult cats (1-7 years)** typically do well on two meals a day, breakfast and dinner roughly 12 hours apart. Some people do three meals; some free-feed; both can work. The argument for scheduled meals is that it makes portion control easier, lets you notice appetite changes (a key health indicator), and reduces grazing-related obesity. The argument for free-feeding is that it more closely matches a cat's natural pattern of eating many small meals, and works better for households where you're not always home at meal times. A middle-ground option that works for many people: measured portions divided into a morning meal, evening meal, and a small midday snack from a timed feeder or puzzle feeder.

**Senior cats (7+ years, though some vets use 10+ as the threshold)** need re-evaluation. Some seniors lose weight and need more calories or higher-protein food to maintain muscle mass. Others gain weight as they slow down and need less. Many develop conditions — kidney disease, diabetes, hyperthyroidism — that come with their own dietary requirements. So senior nutrition is less about a fixed rule and more about regular vet checkups (twice yearly is recommended) to catch changes early and adjust accordingly. Senior-formulated foods aren't strictly necessary for healthy older cats, but they're often slightly easier to digest.

**About body condition:** more important than what the scale says is what your cat looks and feels like. You should be able to feel ribs without pressing hard, see a slight waist when looking down from above, and see a slight tuck in the belly from the side. If you can't feel ribs through a layer of fat, your cat is overweight. If you can see ribs from across the room, they're underweight. Either way, talk to your vet — feline obesity is way more common than people realize and contributes to diabetes, joint problems, and a shorter life.

**Practical tips:** measure food rather than eyeballing it (a cheap measuring cup or a digital kitchen scale), feed in slow-feeder bowls if your cat inhales food and throws up, and remember that treats count toward total calories — a few treats here and there add up faster than people expect. The 90/10 rule is a decent guideline: at least 90% of daily calories from balanced cat food, no more than 10% from treats.
